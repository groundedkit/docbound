Looking back at our move from MongoDB to PostgreSQL

When we started the migration in January, the orders collection held roughly 40 million documents and was growing by about two million a month. Most of our pain came from reporting queries: the analytics team needed joins across orders, customers and invoices, and every new report meant another hand-written aggregation pipeline that someone had to maintain. We also wanted proper foreign keys, because we had found more than 8,000 orders pointing at customers that no longer existed.

We decided early that we would not attempt a big-bang cutover. Instead we ran both databases side by side for eleven weeks. Every write went to MongoDB first and was then copied to PostgreSQL by a change data capture pipeline built on Debezium and Kafka. A nightly reconciliation job compared row counts and checksums between the two stores, and we did not move on to the next phase until the job had reported zero differences for seven nights in a row.

The schema design took longer than anyone expected. Orders in MongoDB had grown to contain deeply nested line items, discounts and shipping details, some of them in three different historical formats. We ended up with eleven tables, and we kept a single JSONB column called legacy_attributes for fields that nobody could explain. That column was supposed to be temporary, but at the time of writing it still holds data for about 3 percent of orders.

Performance surprised us in both directions. Simple lookups by order ID became slightly slower, from a median of 1.8 milliseconds to 2.4 milliseconds, mostly because of the extra joins. Reporting queries, on the other hand, became dramatically faster: the monthly revenue report went from 14 minutes to 40 seconds once it could use proper indexes and a materialized view refreshed every hour.

The riskiest moment was the switch of reads from MongoDB to PostgreSQL. We moved traffic gradually using a feature flag, starting with 1 percent of read requests and doubling the share every day while watching error rates and latency. At 32 percent we discovered that one rarely used endpoint relied on MongoDB's case-insensitive collation for email lookups, which PostgreSQL did not replicate. We paused the rollout for four days, added a citext column with its own index, and resumed.

Writes were switched last, during a planned maintenance window on a Sunday morning. The window was scheduled for two hours, and the actual cutover took 47 minutes, most of which was spent waiting for the final reconciliation run. We kept MongoDB running in read-only mode for a further thirty days as a fallback, but we never needed it, and the cluster was finally shut down at the end of May.

If we did it again, we would invest in the reconciliation tooling from the very first day instead of building it in week three, because it was the single thing that gave everyone confidence to keep going. We would also involve the analytics team in the schema design much earlier; several of the eleven tables had to be reshaped after they tried to write their first reports against them. Finally, we underestimated the cost of running two databases in parallel: the extra infrastructure added about 6,500 euros per month for the duration of the migration.
